# Agent Notes: Avoiding AI-Patterned Writing

_Last updated: June 18, 2026._

## Purpose

These notes preserve the style findings from the Epicurean paradox editing pass, along with Will's recommendations. Use them when writing or revising project documents, especially theological essays, critiques, treatises, and reflective material.

The goal is not to trick AI detectors. The goal is better writing: more human, more direct, more honest, less canned, and less trapped in generated rhythms.

## Research Findings

Online research on AI writing tells pointed to several recurring patterns:

- AI writing often sounds overly polished, symmetrical, and impersonal.
- It tends to use predictable transitions, repeated structures, and tidy conclusions.
- It can lean on generic phrases such as "at its core," "delve into," or broad summary scaffolding.
- It often repeats sentence shapes across a document, even when the meaning changes.
- It may over-explain, over-list, or organize every thought into a clean framework.
- It can flatten emotional weight by making pain, conflict, or uncertainty sound too resolved.
- Style detectors based on perplexity, burstiness, or similar signals are unreliable and can falsely flag human writing, so detector-chasing is the wrong goal.

The strongest practical lesson: edit for lived clarity, concrete thought, and natural variation. Do not optimize for a detector score.

## Will's Recommendations

Will specifically identified these as AI-smell patterns:

- "not X / but Y"
- "is not X / is Y"
- "does not X / does Y"

These contrast templates can be useful in moderation, but repeated across a document they start to sound mechanical. They often create the feeling of a generated aphorism instead of a human argument.

When revising, scan for these forms and ask whether the contrast is necessary. If the contrast is just style, rewrite it.

## Practical Editing Rules

Use plain statements where possible.

Weak pattern:

> The answer is not an abstract idea. It is a person.

Stronger option:

> A Person stands at the center of the answer.

Use necessary negation without fear.

Some negations belong in the argument, especially when quoting an objection, naming an error, or preserving theological precision. Do not remove every "not," "cannot," or "does not." Remove the repeated template when it becomes a cadence.

Break repetitive symmetry.

If several paragraphs follow the same structure, revise some into direct prose, some into shorter sentences, and some into fuller explanation. A human essay can breathe.

Avoid over-tidy conclusions.

Theological writing should be clear, but suffering, mystery, repentance, and faith should not sound shrink-wrapped. Leave room for reverence, lament, and honest limitation.

Prefer concrete verbs.

Use verbs like "reveals," "exposes," "receives," "answers," "bears," "restores," "honors," and "judges" when they fit. Avoid generic filler verbs that merely arrange ideas.

Cut generic scaffolding.

Watch for:

- "This essay will explore..."
- "It is important to note..."
- "At its core..."
- "In today's world..."
- "This raises important questions..."
- "Ultimately..."

These phrases are not always wrong, but they often signal that the prose is warming up instead of speaking.

## Revision Checklist

Before finishing a document, check for:

- Repeated "not X / but Y" contrasts.
- Repeated "is not X / is Y" contrasts.
- Repeated "does not X / does Y" contrasts.
- Overuse of parallel bullets or numbered explanations.
- Too many sentences with the same length and rhythm.
- Abstract nouns where concrete verbs would be stronger.
- Conclusions that sound too neat for the emotional weight of the subject.
- Polished phrasing that no longer sounds like someone thinking carefully before God.

## When To Keep A Pattern

Keep a contrast if it is doing real work:

- The document is quoting or preserving a known argument.
- The negation protects theological accuracy.
- The contrast clarifies a genuine distinction.
- The sentence would become vague or weaker without it.

Even then, avoid stacking the same structure several times in a row.
