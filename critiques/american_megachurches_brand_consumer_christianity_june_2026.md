# A Loving But Honest Critique of American Megachurches and Branded Consumer Christianity

_Written for the BibleAnalysis project. Public context current to June 2026._

## Scope

This is not a critique of every large church, every church with excellent music, every church with a cafe, every church bookstore, every multisite church, every large auditorium, every worship band, or every pastor whose sermons reach beyond one local congregation.

Large churches can do real good. Many feed the hungry, serve single parents, plant churches, support missionaries, run recovery ministries, provide counseling, care for children, mobilize volunteers, and make room for people who would never walk into a small traditional church. Some large churches are full of ordinary saints quietly loving Jesus, serving neighbors, and trying to be faithful.

That should be honored.

This critique is aimed at a visible pattern in American megachurch life: Christianity packaged as a brand, worship produced as an event, church experienced as a consumer environment, pastors elevated as platform personalities, and the gathered body of Christ shaped by the logic of growth, image, money, scale, and market appeal.

The issue is not size by itself.

The issue is what size can hide, reward, and normalize.

## The June 2026 Context

The Hartford Institute for Religion Research defines megachurches as large Protestant congregations with 2,000 or more people in weekly worship attendance, counting adults and children across locations. Hartford notes that these churches commonly feature a charismatic senior minister, contemporary worship formats, active programming, multiple ministries, small-group systems, and complex organizational structures.

Hartford's Megachurch 2020 report estimated roughly 1,750 megachurches in the United States before the pandemic. The report also described how megachurch influence travels far beyond attendance numbers: through conferences, books, coaching networks, podcasts, social media, leadership models, worship music, and personal networks of church leaders.

That influence matters in June 2026 because American congregational life is recalibrating after years of disruption. Hartford and Faith Communities Today reported in April 2026 that median in-person worship attendance had risen to 70, the first positive gain in 25 years of tracking, while also warning that the rebound is uneven and that larger congregations are generally more likely to grow than smaller ones.

Barna's research adds another layer. In its Spiritually Open project, Barna found that Americans remain much warmer toward Jesus than toward many institutional or celebrity expressions of Christianity. Barna reported that 71 percent of U.S. teens and adults view Jesus positively, while views of celebrity, mega, and famous representatives of the faith are "decidedly negative." In separate research, Barna found only 57 percent of U.S. adults agreed at least somewhat that a pastor is a trustworthy source of wisdom.

This is the environment American megachurches occupy: many people are still spiritually open, but institutional trust is fragile; attendance is recovering unevenly, but larger churches often benefit; and the most visible forms of American Christianity can easily become the public face of Jesus to people who have never truly encountered Him.

That is a serious stewardship.

## The Central Danger

The central danger is not that a church becomes large.

The central danger is that a church becomes marketable.

A large church can still be a church. A polished church can still be a church. A church with lights, screens, a cafe, a bookstore, a parking team, children's check-in, a good website, a worship album, and clean signage can still be a church.

But when the total experience teaches people to relate to Christianity as a product, the church is being spiritually deformed.

Jesus said:

> "My Father's house into a marketplace" - John 2:16, BSB

The early church, by contrast, is described as devoted to the apostles' teaching, fellowship, breaking bread, and prayer (Acts 2:42).

Those two passages create a holy tension for the American megachurch.

The church is not a marketplace.

The church is a people devoted to teaching, fellowship, bread, and prayer.

If the lobby, the lights, the coffee, the bookstore, the stage, the pastor's face, the worship brand, the social clips, the merch table, and the giving system become stronger signals than repentance, communion, prayer, holiness, service, and Jesus Christ Himself, then something has gone wrong.

## 1. The Cafe Is Not the Sin. The Marketplace Instinct Is.

A cafe can be hospitality. Coffee can help people linger. A bookstore can help people learn. A well-designed lobby can make visitors feel less anxious. Excellence is not the enemy of holiness.

But the marketplace instinct is spiritually dangerous.

The marketplace instinct asks:

- How do we capture attention?
- How do we create a sticky experience?
- How do we increase engagement?
- How do we move people through the funnel?
- How do we make the brand feel premium?
- How do we keep them coming back?

Those questions may have limited practical value in organizing people. But they cannot be allowed to become the church's imagination.

When the first emotional impression of church is retail, the soul learns something. When the lobby feels like a lifestyle brand, the soul learns something. When every wall, cup, shirt, sign, app, sermon graphic, conference banner, and worship release carries the same brand grammar, the soul learns something.

It learns to consume.

The cafe is not the sin. The danger is when fellowship becomes atmosphere, welcome becomes customer experience, and the house of God feels like a religious mall with better music.

## 2. Worship Can Become Production Instead of Surrender

Excellent music is not wrong.

Skilled musicians are not wrong.

Lights, screens, audio engineering, rehearsal, and thoughtful service planning are not wrong.

The danger is when worship becomes emotional production.

In many megachurch environments, worship is designed with extraordinary precision: dark room, bright stage, professional band, swelling pads, LED walls, carefully placed transitions, timed altar calls, camera-ready moments, and language that makes the congregation feel they are participating in something huge.

That can help people focus.

It can also train people to need atmosphere in order to worship.

If worship requires volume, lights, a certain sound, a certain emotional rise, a certain leader, a certain song catalog, and a certain cinematic intensity, then the church may be forming people who can be moved by religious aesthetics but struggle to pray in silence, repent in truth, serve in obscurity, or sing when nothing feels impressive.

The presence of God is not manufactured by production value.

The Spirit of God does not need a build.

## 3. The Pastor Can Become the Product

Hartford's megachurch research names a charismatic, authoritative senior minister as one common feature of megachurch life. That does not mean every such pastor is corrupt. Some are sincere, gifted, sacrificial, and deeply loving.

But the structure itself creates danger.

When thousands of people gather around one preacher's voice, face, personality, leadership style, books, podcasts, conferences, quotes, clips, and personal story, the pastor can slowly become the church's product.

People stop saying, "I belong to this local body."

They start saying, "I go to that pastor's church."

The pastor becomes brand anchor, chief visionary, culture carrier, fundraiser, executive, media figure, and spiritual father to people he does not personally know.

That is too much weight for one human soul.

Peter wrote:

> "Be shepherds of God's flock" - 1 Peter 5:2, BSB

Peter also warns shepherds against greed and domination, calling them instead to be examples to the flock (1 Peter 5:2-3).

Shepherding is not platform management.

A pastor is not a mascot, CEO, guru, celebrity, founder, or spiritual entrepreneur.

A pastor is a shepherd.

## 4. Scale Can Replace Shepherding With Systems

Megachurches often create impressive systems: small groups, serving teams, assimilation pipelines, care forms, pastoral departments, automated emails, leadership cohorts, app notifications, giving dashboards, and follow-up processes.

Some of this is necessary at scale.

But systems are not the same thing as shepherding.

Systems can move people. Shepherds know people.

Systems can track attendance. Shepherds notice absence.

Systems can assign volunteers. Shepherds discern wounds.

Systems can generate reports. Shepherds pray by name.

Systems can create the appearance of care while hiding the absence of care.

The larger a church becomes, the more brutally honest it must be about whether its people are actually known. If the only people truly known are leaders, donors, musicians, influencers, staff, and high-capacity volunteers, then the church has built a platform with a crowd around it, not a body where each member is honored.

## 5. Metrics Can Disguise Spiritual Immaturity

Megachurches are often fluent in metrics: weekend attendance, baptisms, giving, livestream views, small-group signups, volunteer hours, sermon downloads, app engagement, campus growth, conversion responses, and first-time guest cards.

Numbers can matter. The book of Acts counted people. Churches should care whether people are coming, being baptized, receiving care, and serving.

But numbers are terrible gods.

Attendance does not prove discipleship.

Giving does not prove generosity.

Serving does not prove maturity.

Emotional worship does not prove surrender.

Brand loyalty does not prove union with Christ.

A church can gather thousands and still form shallow people. It can baptize many and fail to teach them to obey Jesus. It can inspire volunteers while burning them out. It can create excitement while leaving secret sin, loneliness, addiction, debt, resentment, and fear untouched.

The question is not, "How many came?"

The question is, "Are people becoming free in Jesus Christ?"

## 6. Multisite Can Quietly Become Franchise Christianity

Multisite ministry can be practical. It can help growing churches use resources wisely. It can serve neighborhoods that need faithful ministry. It can allow gifted teachers to bless more people.

But multisite can also turn the local church into a franchise.

One brand.

One teaching feed.

One visual identity.

One central leadership culture.

One sermon streamed across many rooms.

One name expanding into new markets.

The danger is that the congregation becomes a campus, the pastor becomes a broadcast, local shepherds become site managers, and the church becomes portable religious infrastructure.

The New Testament does not picture the church as a scalable content delivery system. It pictures local people gathered under Christ, known by shepherds, sharing bread, bearing burdens, confessing sins, caring for widows, practicing discipline, and loving one another in embodied life.

If multisite weakens local shepherding, local accountability, local prayer, and local communion, then efficiency has started to cost too much.

## 7. The Bookstore Can Turn Formation Into Consumption

Books can bless the church. Good books can teach, correct, comfort, and deepen faith. There is nothing wrong with making helpful resources available.

But the bookstore can also become a symbol of something diseased.

Buy the pastor's book.

Buy the conference ticket.

Buy the study guide.

Buy the worship album.

Buy the devotional.

Buy the merch.

Buy the leadership curriculum.

Buy the marriage course.

Buy the student camp package.

Buy the branded Bible tabs, tumbler, hoodie, and journal.

Again, not every sale is wrong. But the church must tremble before the possibility that it is teaching people to purchase spiritual identity.

Paul wrote:

> "peddle the word of God for profit" - 2 Corinthians 2:17, BSB

The Word of God must not be treated like premium content.

The gifts of God must not be merchandised as access points to belonging.

## 8. Branding Can Protect Image More Than Truth

Branding is not automatically evil. Clarity can serve mission. Good design can reduce confusion. A consistent name, logo, and communication style can help people find their way.

But branding becomes idolatrous when image protection becomes more important than truth.

When the church has a brand to protect, it can become tempted to hide abuse, soften scandal, manage victims, control narratives, overuse nondisclosure agreements, punish whistleblowers, and frame every criticism as an attack on the mission.

This is especially dangerous in churches built around a founder, senior pastor, worship brand, or reputation for excellence.

The bigger the platform, the stronger the temptation to treat sin as a public-relations problem.

But sin is not a PR problem.

Sin is reality that must be brought into the light of truth.

If a church is more concerned with whether people still trust the brand than whether wounded people are safe, heard, and honored, the church has betrayed its Lord.

## 9. Volunteers Can Become the Hidden Labor Behind the Experience

Megachurches often depend on large volunteer armies. Parking teams, greeters, ushers, children's workers, youth leaders, musicians, production teams, small-group leaders, security teams, cafe teams, setup teams, teardown teams, and prayer teams make the whole machine run.

Many volunteers serve joyfully.

But a church must ask whether it is forming servants or exploiting devotion.

When volunteers are constantly told that the weekend experience depends on them, that excellence honors God, that empty slots are a spiritual emergency, and that serving the vision is the path to significance, they can become spiritually exhausted while believing burnout is faithfulness.

Jesus does not need His people to be used up by a brand.

The church should teach people to serve from rest in Christ, not from anxiety that the experience will collapse without them.

## 10. The Poor Can Become Outreach Rather Than Family

Many megachurches do significant outreach to the poor. That is good.

But there is a difference between serving the poor as a ministry category and becoming a church where the poor are family.

A sprawling campus near major traffic corridors, built around cars, screens, programs, paid staff, polished environments, and middle-class expectations can unintentionally signal who the church is for.

The poor may receive help.

But do they belong?

The immigrant may receive a backpack drive.

But are they known?

The single mother may receive assistance.

But is she part of the household?

The addict may attend recovery.

But is he honored as a brother?

The disabled person may be accommodated.

But are they needed?

Outreach can become another brand expression unless the people served become actual members of the family, with dignity, voice, presence, and reciprocal love.

## 11. The Church Can Become a Place to Attend Instead of a People to Belong To

The megachurch model often makes attendance easy.

That can be a gift.

But it can also make belonging optional.

People can arrive late, sit in the dark, enjoy the band, hear the message, leave during the final song, get coffee, buy a book, and disappear without anyone knowing whether they are alive, suffering, repentant, lonely, faithful, or falling apart.

That is not the life of the body.

The church is not an event people attend.

The church is a people who belong to Jesus and to one another.

If a church makes it easy to consume but difficult to be known, it has not solved the problem of accessibility. It has created religious anonymity at scale.

## 12. Excellence Can Become a Cover for Prayerlessness

Some megachurches are organizationally excellent. They know how to plan, execute, communicate, design, recruit, produce, and scale.

That can be useful.

But excellence can hide prayerlessness.

A church can run without depending on God if the machinery is strong enough. The sermon can be good enough, the music moving enough, the child care smooth enough, the brand trusted enough, the parking clear enough, the giving system frictionless enough, and the leadership pipeline effective enough.

But a church that can function without prayer is in grave danger.

The early church devoted itself to prayer.

Not production.

Not brand.

Not scale.

Prayer.

The American megachurch must ask: If the Holy Spirit withdrew, how long would the weekend experience continue to look successful?

## What Repentance Could Look Like

Repentance could look like churches doing the following:

- Publicly re-centering Jesus Christ, the cross, resurrection, repentance, prayer, communion, and eternal life as the heart of the church.
- Treating size as stewardship, not proof of blessing.
- Reducing the visibility of the senior pastor's personal brand.
- Practicing real plurality of leadership, with elders who can correct senior leaders without fear.
- Creating external accountability structures, independent financial review, and credible processes for abuse reports.
- Measuring fruit beyond attendance, giving, baptisms, and engagement.
- Asking whether ordinary members are known, shepherded, visited, corrected, prayed for, and loved.
- Making resources freely available wherever possible, especially for people without money.
- Refusing to let the bookstore, cafe, merch, conference, or worship brand become central to the experience of belonging.
- Building worship that can survive without spectacle.
- Teaching people to pray, repent, forgive, serve, suffer, and abide in Jesus when nothing feels emotionally impressive.
- Protecting volunteers from burnout and refusing to use spiritual language to pressure people into overextension.
- Making the poor, disabled, elderly, immigrant, recovering addict, and socially inconvenient person part of the family, not merely the outreach strategy.
- Letting local campuses have real local shepherds, not only managers of a centralized brand.
- Confessing when excellence has become pride, when growth has become greed, and when image has been protected at the expense of truth.

This repentance would be painful because it would feel like becoming less impressive.

But the church was never called to be impressive.

The church was called to be faithful.

## A Word of Love to American Megachurches

You are not wrong to be large.

You are not wrong to use technology.

You are not wrong to sing with skill.

You are not wrong to help people feel welcome.

You are not wrong to make coffee.

You are not wrong to publish books.

You are not wrong to organize well.

You are not wrong to reach many people.

But you cannot turn Jesus into a brand.

You cannot turn worship into a concert.

You cannot turn shepherding into systems.

You cannot turn fellowship into atmosphere.

You cannot turn discipleship into content consumption.

You cannot turn pastors into celebrities.

You cannot turn volunteers into fuel.

You cannot turn the poor into marketing.

You cannot turn the house of God into a marketplace and call it excellence.

The call is not to become smaller.

The call is not to become uglier, clumsier, poorer in quality, or suspicious of every tool.

The call is to become more deeply Christian.

## A Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy on Your church.

Free us from spectacle without surrender.

Free us from growth without discipleship.

Free us from excellence without prayer.

Free us from branding without truth.

Free us from platforms without shepherds.

Free us from markets disguised as ministry.

Teach us to welcome without selling.

Teach us to worship without performing.

Teach us to lead without exalting ourselves.

Teach us to serve without using people.

Teach us to build only what love can inhabit.

Bring every image-managed sin into the light. Bring every wounded person under Your mercy. Bring every idol to the cross.

Make Your church faithful, humble, prayerful, honest, generous, and free.

Amen.
